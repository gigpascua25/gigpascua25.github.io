Margin leakage analysis

An analysis of 51,290 e-commerce order lines that tests five explanations for four years of flat profit margin and finds deep discounting most strongly associated with losses.

Team
Team of six.
My contribution
I investigated whether discounting explains the profit leakage, added a comparable case from Ralph Lauren, and presented the two slides on the discount findings. Data preparation, the other hypothesis tests and the final recommendations were team work.
Tools
Spreadsheets, with AI-assisted exploration guided by the bootcamp's structured prompt sheet.
Data
A global e-commerce retailer, 2014–2017: 51,290 order lines, 25,728 orders and 1,970 returned orders across 165 countries.

Business question

Sales grew from about $2.26M in 2014 to $4.30M in 2017, but profit margin barely moved, from 11.0% to 11.7%. The business is profitable. The team asked where profit is leaking, what appears to drive it, and what management can realistically do about it.

Approach

  1. The team joined orders, returns and regional leads, removed 63 duplicate return rows, rebuilt a broken region join for Canada, and confirmed that totals still reconciled to the source to the cent.
  2. Five explanations for the low margin were tested: discounting, product mix, geography, returns and customer segment. My part was the discounting analysis.
  3. Margin was measured by discount band to find where sales begin to lose money.
  4. The results were turned into a staged recommendation, with an illustrative scenario and a regional pilot.

Findings

The two findings below are my part of the analysis, which I presented in the team's deck.

  • Discounting costs considerably more than returns. Return-related losses total about $136,600, while sales discounted by more than 20% lost about $815,000, more than half of total profit. The data records no reasons for returns, so returns are a secondary opportunity.
  • Margin falls sharply as discounts deepen. Full-price sales retain about 25 cents of every dollar. Margin crosses zero at a discount of roughly 20–30% and declines steeply beyond 30%. Losses begin in the 21–30% band, which is why the proposed approval threshold is set at 30%: routine promotions continue unaffected while the largest losses are controlled.

The team's other tests did not support the alternative explanations. All 17 sub-categories earn 21–32% at full price, and the three customer segments all fall between 11.5% and 12% margin.

Recommendations

  • Require approval for discounts above 30%, with a documented exception process.
  • Pilot the policy for 90 days in Western Asia, a region with meaningful sales volume and a negative margin.
  • Roll the policy out company-wide only if profit improves without an unacceptable fall in sales volume.

As a comparable case, I added Ralph Lauren, which deliberately reduced markdowns from 2017 and expanded margins without losing comparable-store sales. As a premium brand, its customers may be less price-sensitive, which is a further reason to pilot before rolling out.

Limitations

  • Discounts were not randomized, so every finding is an association, not proof of cause.
  • The data has no cost-of-goods or currency column. Findings lead with percentages, and dollar totals are treated as close estimates.
  • Shipping costs sit outside the profit figure. They shift every line equally and do not explain the leakage.
