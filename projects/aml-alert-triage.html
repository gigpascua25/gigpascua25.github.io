Customer activity versus declared income

An analysis for a bank's anti-money-laundering team that compares each customer's 2025 transactions with the income declared at account opening. Synthetic bootcamp dataset.

Team
Team of six.
My contribution
I wrote the executive summary and business context for the whole project, and carried out Investigation 4, the declared-income analysis shown on this page. Teammates carried out the other four investigations: the large-payment rule, payments just below the reporting threshold, dormant accounts, and alert prioritization.
AI assistance
I used AI tools to help draft the code, and reviewed and tested it myself.
Tools
SQL on Google BigQuery; Python with pandas, matplotlib and seaborn.
Data
A synthetic anti-money-laundering scenario provided by the bootcamp: 10,000 customers, 46,793 payments and 2,618 alerts in 2025.

Business question

In the scenario, a bank's Head of AML Transaction Monitoring leads 24 investigators. In 2025, five automated rules raised 2,618 alerts; 1,623 were investigated and 995 were never opened. With no additional headcount available, the team's question was which alerts should be reviewed first.

My investigation addressed a narrower question: does the amount each customer moved in 2025 resemble the income they declared when opening their account?

Approach

  1. Wrote a BigQuery query that aggregates customers, accounts and outgoing payments to one row per customer, with money moved expressed as a percentage of declared income, and confirmed that the row count equals the customer count.
  2. Standardized the free-text occupation field. 18 spellings, such as "student", "STUDENT" and " Student", were mapped to 5 occupations, with counts checked before and after.
  3. Compared each customer only with customers who declared the same occupation, using the median. A single ranking across all customers would mainly surface business owners, whose personal income says little about their account activity.
  4. Joined the results to the alert data to test whether the existing rules already capture these customers.

Findings

  • The largest gaps are among students and unemployed customers. The median student moved 7.7% of declared income and the median unemployed customer 6.0%, compared with 0.8% for professionals and 0.4% for business owners. This is consistent with a documented money-mule recruitment pattern, but legitimate activity, such as a student passing on family support, looks the same. The measure identifies accounts for review, not wrongdoing.
  • The measure should not become a new alert rule. About three in four of the 469 customers who moved more than their declared income had already raised an alert, compared with 4% of all customers. A new rule would add workload with little additional coverage.
  • The customers who were missed share one characteristic. About 120 of them never raised an alert, and none made a single payment of PHP 500,000 or more. The largest rule assesses one payment at a time, so customers whose individual payments stay below that threshold are never flagged.

Limitations

  • Declared income is recorded by the customer at account opening, is not verified, and may be several years old.
  • Money moved includes transfers between a customer's own accounts.
  • The data does not state the period that declared income covers, so only comparisons between occupation groups are meaningful.
  • None of this identifies wrongdoing. The output is a list of accounts that merit human review.
