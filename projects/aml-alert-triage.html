Spending against declared income

A check of whether bank customers' 2025 transactions match the income they declared, written for an anti-money-laundering team with more alerts than investigators.

Role
Team of six. I wrote the executive summary and business context, and Investigation 4, shown here.
Tools
SQL on Google BigQuery, Python with pandas, matplotlib and seaborn.
Data
A synthetic anti-money-laundering scenario from the bootcamp: 10,000 customers, 46,793 payments and 2,618 alerts in 2025.

The question

The bank's Head of AML Transaction Monitoring has 24 investigators. In 2025 five automatic rules raised 2,618 alerts; 1,623 were opened and 995 were never looked at. She cannot hire, so the team asked which alerts should be looked at first.

My investigation asked something simpler: does the money each customer moved look anything like the income they declared when they opened the account?

What I did

  1. Wrote one BigQuery query that rolls customers, accounts and outgoing payments up to one row per customer, with money moved as a share of declared income. Then I checked the grain: rows equal customers.
  2. Cleaned the hand-typed job field. 18 spellings, such as "student", "STUDENT" and " Student", became 5 jobs, checked before and after.
  3. Compared each customer only with others who declared the same job, using the median. Ranking everyone together just produces a list of business owners, whose personal income says little about their payments.
  4. Joined the result to the alert data to see whether the existing rules already catch these customers.

What I found

  • The gap is largest where few people expect it. A typical student moved 7.7% of declared income and a typical unemployed customer 6.0%, against 0.8% for professionals. Recruiting students and people out of work to move money is a documented pattern, but a student passing on family money looks the same, so this marks accounts for review, not people.
  • It should not become a sixth alert rule. About three quarters of the 469 customers who moved more than their declared income had already raised an alert, against 4% of all customers. A new rule would add work without adding much coverage.
  • The ones who slipped through share one trait. About 120 never raised an alert, and none of them made a single payment of PHP 500,000 or more. The largest rule looks at one payment at a time, so payments that each stay under the line never trigger it.

Limits I flagged

  • Declared income was written down by the customer at onboarding, never verified, and may be years old.
  • Money moved includes transfers between a customer's own accounts.
  • The data does not say what period declared income covers, so only comparisons between job groups are meaningful.
  • Nothing here identifies wrongdoing. It produces a list of accounts worth a human look.
